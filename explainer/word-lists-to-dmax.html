isodiv · how the numbers are made

From word lists to Dmax

Every number in the results table comes from one kind of raw material: short lists of basic words, written in a coarse phonetic alphabet. This page follows real ASJP lists from Japanese, Okinawan (Naha), Yaeyaman (Hateruma), English, German and Basque through each step. Every figure below is computed live in your browser from the actual ASJP v21 forms.

The pipeline now measures distance with PMI-LDND, which aligns words using sound correspondences learned from the data (§3). Switch to the original LDND to compare any step.

What an ASJP word list is

The Automated Similarity Judgment Program database (v21) holds about 11,500 word lists. Each one is a doculect: a single documented variety from a single source. The same language can appear more than once. JAPANESE and JAPANESE_2 are both Standard Japanese (Glottocode nucl1643), transcribed from different sources.

Every list covers the same 40 concepts. These are the most stable items of an original Swadesh-100, chosen because they are least often replaced or borrowed:

Words are written in ASJPcode, a deliberately coarse ASCII alphabet of 7 vowels and 34 consonants. Coarse is on purpose: transcriptions from very different traditions (a Japanese grammar, a Bantu field survey) become comparable, at the cost of phonetic detail. Many distinctions collapse; all front mid vowels, for example, are e.

2

Comparing one word with one word

For one concept, take the two languages’ words and count the fewest single-segment edits (substitute, insert, delete) that turn one into the other. That count is the Levenshtein distance. Divide by the length of the longer word, so long words aren’t penalized for being long. The result is LDN: 0 for identical words, 1 for words with nothing in the right place.

LDN(a, b) = Levenshtein(a, b) / max(|a|, |b|)

LDN knows nothing about sound change. Japanese hi and Hateruma p3 ‘fire’ are regular cognates (Proto-Japonic *pi), yet score 1.0, while chance lookalikes can score low. This method gives up cognate judgments to get a measurement that runs on all 11,500 lists without an expert. §3 fixes the sound-change blindness; §4 deals with chance.

3

Learned sound correspondences: PMI

Levenshtein charges the same for every changed segment. The pipeline instead learns what each pairing is worth. It takes word pairs that are probably cognate (same meaning, from lists that are already fairly similar), aligns them, and counts how often each pair of segments lines up, compared with how often it would by chance. That ratio, on a log scale, is the pointwise mutual information:

PMI(a, b) = log p(a aligned with b) / (q(a) · q(b))

Positive means the two segments line up more often than chance, negative means less. Identical segments score high. Regular correspondences score near zero or a little below, even when they sound nothing alike. Pairings that cognates never show score strongly negative. No phonology goes in: the scores below were learned from 3,939 similar ASJP list pairs, re-estimated until stable (after Jäger 2013, 2018).

Words are then aligned to maximize the summed score, with gaps costing −2.49 to open and −1.71 to extend. The total S(a, b) becomes a distance comparable to LDN by comparing it with each word’s score against itself:

d(a, b) = 1 − 2·S(a, b) / (S(a, a) + S(b, b))

Each column shows its score: a darker cell is a stronger correspondence; gaps show their penalty.

Word by word, the change is often modest. Japanese Ci and Hateruma c3 ‘blood’ go from LDN 1.00 to 0.65, because tʃ : ts is a common correspondence. Japanese hi and Hateruma p3 ‘fire’ stay at about 1: h : p scores near zero, but i : ə counts against them.

The bigger change is in words with different meanings. Unrelated segment pairs score strongly negative, so chance pairs land well above 1, while cognates stay moderate. For Japanese–Naha, same-meaning words average 0.80 and different-meaning words 1.66 (ratio 0.48). With LDN those are 0.515 and 0.854 (ratio 0.60). The signal stands out further from the chance baseline, which is what the next step measures.

Swapping this word distance into LDND gives PMI-LDND. Everything after this step is unchanged. On about 5,000 held-out test pairs it separates same-language, same-genus, same-family and unrelated pairs better than LDND at every level, most at deep levels (AUC 0.852 → 0.891).

4

Comparing one list with another: LDND

To compare two doculects, compute LDN for every pair of words between them. Lay them out in a grid: rows are list A’s concepts, columns are list B’s. Then take two averages:

  • LDNsame, the diagonal: words with the same meaning. This is the signal. (Where a list has synonyms, the closest pair counts.)
  • LDNdiff, everything off the diagonal: words with different meanings, e.g. Japanese ‘water’ vs Okinawan ‘fire’. No one thinks these are related, so their average shows how alike two random words from these two languages look by accident.

LDND divides the first by the second. Dividing by the baseline cancels out chance similarity from shared sound systems. Two languages with small inventories and simple CV syllables look alike even when unrelated. Japanese and Okinawan have an LDNdiff around 0.85; English and German, with longer consonant clusters, around 0.91.

LDND = LDNsame / LDNdiff

PMI-LDND is the same ratio with the §3 word distance in every cell. Use the switch at the top to see the grid both ways.

Hover or tap a cell to see the two words. In a related pair the diagonal stands out from the speckle around it. For Japanese vs English it doesn’t, and the distance comes out near 1: the same-meaning words are no more alike than random ones.

Turning distance into similarity

The diversity measure in §7 needs similarities between 0 and 1, not distances. isodiv uses Z = exp(−k · d). The single knob is k, and it is chosen without any clock. 7

From pairs to a lineage: Dmax

A lineage like Japonic has dozens of doculects in ASJP, so it gives a whole matrix of pairwise similarities. Dmax (Leinster & Meckes 2016) reduces that matrix to one number: the effective number of completely distinct lineages in it. 1.0 means everything is one undifferentiated variety; higher means more independent diversity.

It works by letting an optimizer choose how much weight to give each doculect, picking the weights that make the set look as diverse as possible. Near-copies of something already in the set add nothing, so the optimizer gives them zero weight. That is the property the project needs: adding a fourth transcription of Tokyo Japanese, or splitting one language into ten dialect lists, cannot inflate the number. Only genuinely different material can raise it.

8

So what does the results table compare?

Each row of the results is one lineage: every non-excluded ASJP doculect classified under that family (or subfamily node). For each lineage the pipeline computes the PMI-LDND matrix among its own doculects, converts it with kcal, and reports Dmax. The interval comes from re-running with 30 random concepts out of 40, thirty times.

So the comparison is between lineages but the measurement is within each one: “how much internal lexical divergence does this lineage’s documented material contain?” A few values from results/summary.csv:
